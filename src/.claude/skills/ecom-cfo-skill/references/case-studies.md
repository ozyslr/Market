# Ecommerce CFO — Case Studies

> Five real client engagements (anonymized) that illustrate the diagnostic
> frameworks, cash traps, and decision-making processes of ecommerce CFO work.
> These aren't textbook examples — they're messy, emotional, and real.

---

## Case Study 1: "Fluora" — The Art Business Subsidizing Ecommerce

### Background
Fluora is a hybrid business: an art/creative studio that launched a physical
product line on Amazon and Shopify. The founder is a creator first, seller second.
Revenue looked solid. Margins looked fine. But cash was always tight.

### The Diagnosis
We built a **Sources & Uses of Cash waterfall** — not a standard P&L, but a
narrative cash flow that traced every dollar from operations through inventory
through debt.

**The waterfall layers:**
1. Cash after net operations (profit + COGS added back, isolating product business)
2. Equipment activity
3. Inventory activity (COGS sold + net inventory purchases)
4. **Cash after inventory** ← the punchline
5. Credit card & loan activity
6. Art business cash contribution
7. Net cash movement

### What We Found
**June example:**
- P&L showed a $490 operating loss — not great, not catastrophic
- Art business contributed $6,300, bringing total to positive
- Yet the bank account dropped by **$38,000**

Where did the cash go?
- $23,000 in net inventory purchases
- $12,000 in excess credit card payments
- $9,000 in loan payments

**The pattern across 5 months:** Cash after inventory was **negative in 4 of 5
months**. The ecommerce business was profitable on paper but consumed more cash
in inventory than it generated from sales. The art business was subsidizing the
product business, and credit cards + inventory loans were masking the gap.

### The Framework Applied
- **Cash Conversion Cycle:** Extremely long — inventory turns were slow (seasonal/artistic products)
- **Cash Floor:** Repeatedly breached. No formal floor established.
- **Inventory Investment:** Over-ordering relative to velocity. Emotional attachment to full catalog.
- **The Waterfall Insight:** Standard P&L showed "fine." Cash waterfall showed "drowning."

### Key Lessons
1. **Profit ≠ cash.** You can be profitable every month and still run out of cash if inventory investment exceeds cash generation.
2. **The cash waterfall is the real P&L** for product businesses. If you stop at "net profit," you miss the biggest cash drain.
3. **Mixed businesses must be isolated.** The art revenue was masking the product business's cash problem. Without separation, the founder couldn't see the bleed.
4. **Debt fills the gap invisibly.** Credit cards and inventory loans let you keep ordering without feeling the pain — until the debt service itself becomes a cash drain.

> "Good margins, bad cash" = inventory + debt cycle eating the profit.

### Outcome
Implemented formal cash waterfall reporting. Established cash floor. Reduced
inventory orders to match actual velocity. Separated art business cash from
product business cash. Within 4 months, product business was cash-flow neutral
and on track to be self-sustaining.

## Case Study 2: "HomeBase" — The $809K Profit Hold-vs-Sell Decision

### Background
HomeBase is an Amazon-native storage and organization brand. Established, profitable,
lean operations. The founder wanted to know: should I sell this business or keep
running it?

### The Numbers
| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| TTM Revenue | ~$3.2M |
| TTM Profit (SDE) | $809K |
| Estimated Enterprise Value | ~$4M (3.8× TTM SDE) |
| Monthly Distributions | $45,000 |
| Inventory on Hand | $950K |
| COGS as % of Revenue | 25% |
| Cash Conversion Cycle | 145 days |
| Cash Floor (soft) | $200K |
| Cash Floor (hard) | $175K |

### The Hold-vs-Sell Analysis

**Sell scenario:**
- Sale at $4M
- Broker fee (10-15%): ~$500K
- Taxes on gain: ~$700K
- Net proceeds: ~$2.8M
- Invested at 8% annual return: ~$224K/year passive income

**Hold scenario:**
- Annual SDE: $809K
- Annual distributions (at $45K/mo): $540K
- Remaining $269K reinvested in business (inventory, growth)
- IRR on owner's equity: ~22%
- Owner controls timing, growth, and optionality

**The conversation:**
> "You're earning buyer-level 18-25% IRR by holding. A buyer targets 30% IRR —
> that's why the multiple is only 3.8×. You're already getting near-buyer returns
> without giving up control. Why sell for a lump sum when you're already getting
> the returns a buyer would target?"

### Working Capital Analysis
```
CCC: 145 days
COGS: 25% of revenue
Working Capital Drag = (145 ÷ 365) × ($3.2M × 0.25) = $317K
WC Drag as % of Revenue = ~10%
```

With only 25% COGS, the 145-day CCC was **less painful than it looked**. The
working capital drag was ~10% of revenue — real but manageable because the gross
margin structure was so strong.

### Distribution Safety Check
```
Monthly distributions: $45,000
Annual: $540,000
Remaining from SDE: $809K − $540K = $269K for reinvestment and reserves
Cash never dropped below $210K (above $200K soft floor)
Tax reserve: funded separately
```

Distributions were safe. Cash floor maintained. Owner could keep paying themselves
comfortably while growing.

### Key Lessons
1. **IRR framing changes the sell decision.** Most sellers think in multiples ("I'd get 4×"). Reframing as IRR comparison ("you're already earning 22%, why sell for a 30% buyer to capture?") is more honest.
2. **COGS % matters as much as CCC.** A 145-day CCC sounds terrible. But at 25% COGS, the actual cash trapped is only 10% of revenue — very different from 40% COGS at 145 days.
3. **Cash floor discipline enables distributions.** Without a defined floor, the founder either over-distributes (cash crisis) or under-distributes (personal frustration). The floor makes it mechanical.
4. **Inventory as a percentage of enterprise value** ($950K inventory on $4M EV = 24%) — buyers factor this into their offer.

### Outcome
Founder chose to hold. Continued $45K/month distributions. Focused on reducing
CCC by improving inventory turns (target: 100 days). Re-evaluate sell decision
annually or when life circumstances change.

## Case Study 3: "ShipKit" — 44 Overstocked SKUs and $108K in Trapped Cash

### Background
ShipKit sells packaging and shipping supplies on Amazon. Large catalog (~200 SKUs),
established brand, steady revenue. The founder felt "stuck" — profitable but
couldn't invest in new product development or marketing. Cash was always tight
despite decent margins.

### The Inventory Audit
We ran a full SKU audit across the catalog using the grading framework:

**Grading criteria:**
- **A SKUs:** >5% of total sales
- **B SKUs:** 2-5% of total sales
- **C SKUs:** <2% of total sales

**Overstock detection:**
- Calculated months of supply for every SKU
- Flagged anything over 200 days (6.7 months) of supply

### What We Found

| Finding | Count | Value |
|---------|-------|-------|
| Total SKUs audited | ~200 | |
| Overstocked SKUs (200+ days) | 44 | |
| Severely overstocked (365+ days) | 16 | |
| Total cash trapped in overstock | | $108,500 |
| Average overstock per SKU | | $2,466 |

**The 44 overstocked SKUs represented ~$108K in cash** sitting in Amazon warehouses
earning nothing. Sixteen of them had over a year's supply — some had 2-3 years
of inventory at current velocity.

### The Promotion Plan

We built a tiered action plan for each overstocked SKU:

| Days of Supply | Grade | Action | Max Discount |
|---------------|-------|--------|-------------|
| 200-300 days | A/B | Moderate promotion (15-20% off) | Gross margin ÷ 2 |
| 200-300 days | C | Aggressive promotion (25-35% off) | Down to breakeven |
| 300-365 days | Any | Bundle with fast sellers | Variable |
| 365+ days | A/B | Deep discount (30-40% off) | Near COGS |
| 365+ days | C | Liquidate or remove from FBA | Below COGS if needed |

**Key principle:** The COGS for overstocked inventory is **already spent**. Discounting
isn't "losing money" — it's recovering cash you already paid. Even selling at COGS
recovers 100% of the cash investment.

### The Refinements We Made
Kate's (the inventory manager) original workbook was good but needed upgrades:

1. **Months of supply instead of raw days** — more intuitive, accounts for seasonality
2. **Grade by velocity × margin contribution**, not just % of total sales — a high-revenue, low-margin SKU grades differently than a moderate-revenue, high-margin one
3. **Overstock threshold relative to lead time** — 90 days supply with China lead time is minimum safety stock; 90 days with domestic lead time is overstocked
4. **Tiered action plans** — not just "discount" but: promote → bundle → liquidate → write-off, escalating over time

### Key Lessons
1. **Cash trapped in inventory is invisible on the P&L.** The P&L showed profit. The balance sheet showed $108K in idle assets.
2. **Overstocked SKUs don't fix themselves.** Without an audit, those 16 SKUs at 365+ days would sit there for another year, tying up cash.
3. **The grading system makes hard decisions mechanical.** Instead of "which products should we discount?" (emotional), it becomes "all C-grade SKUs with 300+ days supply get 25% off" (systematic).
4. **$108K in trapped cash = new product line, ad investment, or 2+ months of owner distributions.** The opportunity cost of idle inventory is enormous.

### Outcome
Executed 90-day promotion plan. Recovered ~$72K of the $108K within first quarter
through staged discounting. Remaining $36K in deep overstock was bundled,
liquidated, or removed from FBA. Freed cash funded a new product line launch.

## Case Study 4: "LeashCraft" — Brand Identity vs Cash Reality

### Background
LeashCraft sells premium, beautifully designed dog leashes and accessories.
Boutique brand, strong visual identity, loyal customer base. Seasonal product —
summer colors, outdoor themes, beach collections peak March through August.

### The Problem
It was October. LeashCraft had significant inventory of summer-themed products.
The founder **refused to discount** because it would "damage the brand." Her exact
reasoning: "We don't do sales. That's not our brand. Discounting tells customers
to wait for markdowns."

### The Cash Reality
```
Summer collection inventory on hand: ~$35,000 at cost
Projected sell-through at full price (Oct-Mar): 15-20% of remaining stock
Cash tied up through winter: ~$28,000-$30,000
Projected Q4 storage fees (Amazon): ~$2,800 (3-4× normal rate)

Meanwhile:
  New spring collection deposit due in January: $18,000
  Cash balance: $42,000
  Cash floor: $25,000
  Available cash: $17,000
  Shortfall for spring deposit: $1,000 minimum, likely more
```

### The Conversation
The founder was caught between brand identity and cash survival. Classic operator
psychology — the product is an extension of themselves.

**The reframe:**
> "I hear you on brand protection. Let's look at what 'no discount' actually costs:
> $30K in cash trapped through winter, $2,800 in storage fees to hold it, and you
> can't fund your spring collection without taking on debt. The brand protection
> is costing you $33K in cash and your ability to launch new products."

**The compromise:**
- 20% off for "end of season" (brand-appropriate framing, not "clearance")
- Bundle slow summer items with year-round products
- Remove lowest-velocity items from FBA to avoid Q4 storage surge
- Set a calendar: summer items get promoted by August 15, liquidated by Labor Day

### The Framework Applied
- **Seasonal Calendar Discipline:** The root cause was no calendar. Summer items should have been promoted in August, not held into October.
- **Inventory = Cash:** Every day that summer inventory sat unsold, the cash was earning nothing. Worse — it was incurring storage fees.
- **Months of Supply:** Some summer items had 18+ months of supply at winter velocity rates.
- **Operator Psychology:** Brand identity is real. But it has to be weighed against cash reality. Frame discounts as "seasonal transitions," not "sales."

### Key Lessons
1. **Seasonal inventory needs a calendar, not a hope.** If you make summer products, the liquidation date is set before you manufacture.
2. **Brand protection has a cash cost.** You can choose to pay it, but you have to see it clearly.
3. **Framing matters:** "End of season" ≠ "clearance sale." Language preserves brand while freeing cash.
4. **Storage fees punish indecision.** Q4 Amazon storage rates are 3-4× normal. Holding seasonal dead stock through Q4 is paying a premium to lose money.

### Outcome
Ran "end of season" promotion in late October. Recovered $22K of the $35K in
inventory cost. Avoided $1,800 in Q4 storage fees by removing dead stock from FBA.
Funded spring collection deposit without debt. Established permanent seasonal
calendar: promote by August 15, liquidate by September 30, never carry summer
stock into Q4.

## Case Study 5: "ComfortWeave" — Liquidation Math and the Profit vs Cash Paradox

### Background
ComfortWeave sells weighted blankets on Amazon. Had accumulated significant
overstock of a heavy, oversized product line that was declining in velocity.
Storage fees were eating margins. The founder was paralyzed: "If I discount below
cost, I'm losing money on every unit."

### The Misconception
The founder's mental model: selling below COGS = losing money.
The reality: the COGS was already paid months ago.

### The Math

**Scenario A: Hold and sell at full price over 12 months**
```
Units in stock: 800
Full price: $49.99
COGS per unit: $18.00 (already paid)
Monthly velocity at full price: 25 units
Time to clear: 32 months
Monthly storage fees (oversized, Amazon): $3.20/unit = $2,560/month
Total storage cost to clear: $2,560 × 32 = $81,920
Commission + fulfillment per unit: $22.00
Profit per unit: $49.99 − $22.00 − $18.00 = $9.99
Total profit (800 units): $7,992
Net after storage: $7,992 − $81,920 = **($73,928) LOSS**
```

**Scenario B: Aggressive discount to clear in 3 months**
```
Discounted price: $29.99 (40% off)
Monthly velocity at discount: 270 units
Time to clear: ~3 months
Storage fees: $2,560 × 3 = $7,680
Commission + fulfillment per unit: $17.50 (lower commission on lower price)
"Profit" per unit: $29.99 − $17.50 − $18.00 = **($5.51) "loss"**
Total "loss" (800 units): ($4,408)
Net after storage: ($4,408) − $7,680 = **($12,088)**
```

**But here's what actually happens to CASH:**

**Scenario A cash flow:**
```
Cash recovered per unit: $49.99 − $22.00 = $27.99 (COGS already paid — it's sunk)
Total cash recovered: $27.99 × 800 = $22,392
Minus storage: ($81,920)
Net cash: ($59,528) over 32 months
Cash recovered in first 3 months: $27.99 × 75 = $2,099
```

**Scenario B cash flow:**
```
Cash recovered per unit: $29.99 − $17.50 = $12.49 (COGS is sunk)
Total cash recovered: $12.49 × 800 = $9,992
Minus storage: ($7,680)
Net cash: $2,312 over 3 months
PLUS: $14,400/month in cash that would have been tied up in inventory
      is now free for 29 months = reinvestment opportunity
```

### The Paradox
> "Profit tanked but cash flooded."

Discounting below "profitable" levels (below COGS) looks terrible on the P&L.
But the COGS was already spent. Every dollar of revenue above Amazon's variable
fees (commission + fulfillment) is **cash recovery**, not profit generation.

The real comparison:
- **Hold:** Lose $59K in cash over 32 months while inventory rots
- **Liquidate:** Recover $2.3K net cash in 3 months and free up warehouse space + working capital

### The Framework Applied
- **Inventory = Cash:** The $14,400 in COGS sitting in that warehouse was already spent. It's sunk. The only question is how fast you recover it.
- **CCC by Product:** The weighted blankets had an effective CCC of 400+ days. Catastrophic for cash.
- **4-Metric Kill Analysis:** High fulfillment (oversized), high storage (heavy/large), declining velocity, moderate margins only at full price — textbook kill candidate.
- **The Munger Frame:** "Would an investor buying this business choose to manufacture weighted blankets?"

### Key Lessons
1. **COGS is sunk for existing inventory.** Once you've paid for it, the only question is how fast you get cash back. "Selling at a loss" on old inventory is "recovering cash faster."
2. **Storage fees compound the damage.** Holding oversized, slow-moving product on Amazon isn't just tying up cash — it's actively burning cash through storage fees.
3. **Profit is an accounting concept. Cash is survival.** The P&L says you "lost" money on the discount. The bank account says you survived.
4. **Speed of cash recovery matters more than margin on liquidation.** Getting $10K back in 3 months to reinvest beats getting $22K back over 32 months while paying $82K in storage.

> "When you sell something, the amount you spent to buy it — you already paid
> for that a long time ago. So discounting old inventory isn't losing money,
> it's recovering money."

### Outcome
Ran 3-month liquidation campaign. Cleared 750 of 800 units. Recovered $9,400 in
net cash after storage fees. Redirected freed-up cash to a new, lighter product
line with better unit economics and 60-day CCC. The P&L showed a "loss" of
$4K for the quarter. The bank account showed $15K more available cash. Profit
tanked. Cash flooded. The business survived and pivoted.

## Common Patterns Across All Five Cases

| Pattern | Cases | Lesson |
|---------|-------|--------|
| **P&L hides cash reality** | Fluora, HomeBase, ComfortWeave | Always build a cash view alongside the P&L |
| **Inventory is the #1 cash trap** | All five | Every case involved cash trapped in physical product |
| **Emotion blocks decisions** | LeashCraft, ComfortWeave | Brand identity, loss aversion, product attachment |
| **Sunk cost misunderstanding** | ComfortWeave, LeashCraft | Founders confuse "selling at a loss" with "recovering sunk cash" |
| **Cash floor discipline** | HomeBase, Fluora | Having a defined floor prevents crisis decisions |
| **Systematic frameworks beat gut feel** | ShipKit, LeashCraft | Grading systems and calendars make hard decisions mechanical |
| **Debt fills the gap invisibly** | Fluora | Credit cards and loans mask the real cash shortfall |
| **Working capital drag varies** | HomeBase | CCC alone doesn't tell the story — COGS% is the modifier |
